Data platform architect. Data engineering, lakehouse, streaming, and ML.

For more than twenty years I have worked on one problem: making data trustworthy enough to act on. Lately it has a second half: making it safe enough for AI agents to use.

How I got here

I learned the craft at Cognizant in Bangalore, delivering CRM, warehousing, and analytics for clients in financial services, logistics, telecommunications, and media.

At TIAA I worked on the analytics platform behind retirement plan administration. Regulated work, where a wrong number has consequences beyond a broken dashboard.

At Belk I managed a team for the first time. I also wrote my first business case for a platform, which meant arguing for architecture in the language of the people paying for it.

At Kaiser Permanente I built the pharmacy data warehouse for a health plan with more than ten million members. HIPAA change control taught me that traceability is a design constraint, not paperwork.

Since 2020 I have owned a data platform end to end at eHealth: architecture, governance, and SOX compliance on a Snowflake and Databricks lakehouse. The problems I spend my time on are resolving one customer across channels that disagree about who that customer is, making a new source a configuration entry instead of new code, and making governance the default rather than an afterthought.

The next consumer of that data is an AI agent. Agents need data they can trust and cannot misuse. That means the access path should fail closed: the agent carries the caller's identity, and the database decides what that identity can see.

What I believe about this work

A new source should be a configuration entry, not a new codebase. If onboarding the sixth source costs what the first one did, the problem is architectural. Parameterize the ingestion, build reconciliation into every load, and the cost curve flattens.

Choose the design whose failures you can survive. When two approaches are close, the tiebreaker is what happens when you get it wrong. A missed row-level policy denies access. A missed application check leaks data. Let that asymmetry decide the architecture.

Data nobody trusts is worse than no data. Reconciliation, lineage, and a data dictionary somebody reads are not overhead. They are what make the work usable.

Stay close enough to build. I review designs and set standards. I also still write the code and tune the jobs. When I stop doing that, my architecture calls stop being credible.
